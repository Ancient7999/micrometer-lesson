Physics practical · Micrometer screw gauge

Diameter, volume and density of a sphere

Aim

To measure the diameter, volume and density of a given sphere.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this session, students should be able to:

  • Use a micrometer to measure the diameter of solid objects.
  • Use a balance to measure mass.
  • Determine the density of a solid sphere by finding its volume and mass.

Why it matters in medicine

Very small thicknesses

Surgical needles, sutures and membranes are specified to hundredths of a millimetre, the scale a micrometer reads.

Tissue and cell layers

Biomedical labs measure the thickness of tissue sections and cell layers with the same screw principle.

Medical devices

Dental implants, crowns and other devices are made and checked to precise sizes, often with a micrometer.

Theory

A micrometer screw gauge turns rotation into a very small, very even forward movement. One turn of the thimble moves the spindle by a fixed distance, and a scale around the thimble splits that turn into equal parts, so lengths can be read to hundredths of a millimetre.

The parts

Pitch

The distance the spindle moves in one full turn of the thimble. Pitch = distance moved ÷ number of turns.

Head scale

The thimble's circular scale, divided into equal parts (here 50).

Least count

The smallest length the gauge can read: one head-scale division.

Datum line

The horizontal line on the sleeve. The head scale is read where it crosses this line.

L.C = pitchnumber of head-scale divisions
= 0.5 mm50 = 0.01 mm

Here two full turns move the spindle 1 mm, so the pitch is 0.5 mm. Each head-scale division is 0.5 ÷ 50 = 0.01 mm.

Reading the two scales

PSR · pitch scale reading

On the sleeve, at the edge of the thimble. Whole millimetres are marked above the datum line and half millimetres below it. Count the last mark the thimble has uncovered, adding 0.5 mm if a half-mark is showing past the last whole mark.

HSR · head scale reading

The thimble division that lines up with the datum line, counted in divisions. Multiply by the least count to turn it into millimetres.

d = PSR + (HSR × L.C)

One full turn uncovers one more half-millimetre mark; one division moves the spindle 0.01 mm.

Zero error

With the jaws closed gently (by the ratchet) and nothing between them, the sleeve should read 0 and the thimble's 0 should sit on the datum line. If it does, the zero error is nil. If not, the closed reading is the zero error, and it is removed from every reading.

corrected reading = observed reading − zero error

Use the ratchet

Turning the thimble hard can squeeze the object and strain the screw, so the reading comes out too small. The ratchet at the end slips with a click once the spindle touches, so every reading is taken with the same light pressure.

Mean of five

A real sphere is never perfectly round, and a single reading carries reading error. Five readings at different positions, averaged, give a better diameter: mean d = (d₁ + d₂ + d₃ + d₄ + d₅) ÷ 5.

From diameter to density

V = 43π(d2)3

Volume of a sphere, with r = d/2. Put d in cm (mm ÷ 10) to get V in cm³.

ρ = mV

Density: mass in g over volume in cm³, giving g/cm³.

Method

Apparatus

Steps

Good practice
  • Ratchet for the last turn. Spin the thimble to get close, then finish with the ratchet until it clicks two or three times.
  • Look straight at the scale. Read the datum line square-on to avoid parallax.
  • Watch the half-millimetre marks. The marks below the datum line add 0.5 mm. Missing one makes the reading 0.5 mm too small.
  • Lock before reading. The lock stops the spindle moving while the scales are read.
  • Clean jaws, clean sphere. Dust or grease between the faces adds to the reading.

Formula & results

V = 43π(d2)3

volume of the sphere, cm³

ρ = mV

density, g/cm³

Calculation

1
Mean diameter d = (12.70 + 12.71 + 12.69 + 12.72 + 12.70) ÷ 5 = 63.52 ÷ 5 = 12.704 mm
2
Millimetres to centimetres (10 mm = 1 cm) d = 12.704 mm ÷ 10 = 1.2704 cm
3
Radius r = d ÷ 2 = 1.2704 ÷ 2 = 0.6352 cm
4
Volume V = (4/3) π r³ = 4.1888 × 0.6352³ = 4.1888 × 0.25629 = 1.074 cm³
5
Mass from the balance m = 8.38 g
6
Density ρ = m ÷ V = 8.38 ÷ 1.074 = 7.80 g/cm³
✓
Result Volume of the sphere V ≈ 1.074 cm³ Density of the sphere ρ ≈ 7.80 g/cm³ close to the density of steel, about 7.8 g/cm³

Common slips

d left in millimetres

Using 12.704 instead of 1.2704 makes V 1000 times too big (1074 instead of 1.074), so the density comes out 1000 times too small.

Diameter instead of radius

(4/3)π d³ is 8 times (4/3)π r³, because (2r)³ = 8r³. The volume is 8 times too big.

Forgetting the cube

(4/3)π r² has the wrong units (cm²) and here gives 1.69 instead of 1.074.

Density upside down

V ÷ m = 0.128 is volume per gram. Density is mass per volume: m ÷ V.

Missing a half-mark

With a half-millimetre mark showing, PSR is 12.5, not 12. Missing it makes every reading 0.5 mm small.

HSR as millimetres

An HSR of 20 means 20 divisions = 0.20 mm. Adding 20 mm would be far off.
